**More than nine brands in ten get exactly the AI visibility their SEO predicts. The index exists for the other 9%.** Fractl ran 96 prompts, 15 times each, through three AI models and mapped the 9,447 brands that came back against their Ahrefs metrics. [The result](https://searchengineland.com/ai-visibility-index-brands-vanishing-from-ai-search-485057), published this week via Search Engine Land and [MarTech](https://martech.org/ai-visibility-index-shows-which-brands-are-disappearing/): about 5% of brands hold high domain ratings, large keyword portfolios, and millions of organic visits — and barely register in AI answers for their own categories. Aetna, Sephora, Samsung, and Medtronic are on the list.

**Key takeaways**

- The dataset: 96 industry-specific prompts × 15 runs × 3 models (GPT-4o, Gemini 2.5 Flash, Claude Sonnet 4.6) = 4,320 responses, 8,500+ unique brand references, 9,447 brands mapped to Ahrefs domain rating, traffic, referring domains, and keyword volume.
- Over 90% of brands behaved as SEO would predict. 471 brands (~5%) were underrepresented — high authority, few or no mentions — and 377 (~4%) overperformed their SEO footprint.
- The finding with the most operational weight: only 11% of brands appeared in all three models. 77% appeared in exactly one. Notion drew roughly 17x more mentions from Claude than from Gemini.
- Categories diverge: insurance had the most underrepresented incumbents (8%), with Lemonade (213 mentions) beating State Farm (172); travel was tightest, with Booking.com, Airbnb, and Expedia taking about 20% of all sector mentions between them.
- The study's own thesis: what a brand says about itself matters less for AI recall than what the rest of the web has repeatedly said about it.
- The caveat that reframes everything: these are model APIs, with no disclosure of web grounding. This is a measurement of what models have memorized, not of any live AI search product.

## What the index measured

The method is simple enough to replicate, which is part of its value. Fractl wrote 96 prompts across eight industries — travel, healthtech, wellness, insurance, lifestyle, fintech, SaaS, and education — ran each 15 times per model to smooth out run-to-run noise, extracted every brand mentioned, and joined the mention counts to each brand's Ahrefs profile.

| Component | Value |
|---|---|
| Models | GPT-4o, Gemini 2.5 Flash, Claude Sonnet 4.6 |
| Prompts | 96, industry-specific |
| Runs | 15 per prompt per model, 4,320 responses |
| Brands referenced | 8,500+ unique |
| Brands mapped to SEO data | 9,447 |
| Aligned with SEO expectations | over 90% |
| Underrepresented (authority without mentions) | 471 (~5%) |
| Overperforming (mentions without authority) | 377 (~4%) |

The unit of analysis is the gap between two numbers that most marketing teams keep in separate dashboards: traditional search authority and AI mention frequency. For most brands the two agree, which is worth saying plainly because it is the least quoted finding in the study — SEO authority is still the best single predictor of AI visibility, a pattern consistent with the page-level data on [how rankings relate to AI citations](/blog/rankings-and-ai-citations). The index is interesting for the residual: the brands where the proxy breaks.

## The 5% with authority and no recall

The named examples are what will circulate, and they deserve the attention. In insurance — the category with the largest disconnect, 8% of brands underrepresented — Aetna, Cigna, Humana, and Liberty Mutual carry domain ratings above 80 and millions of monthly organic visits, and barely surfaced in their own categories. The mention counts went to digital-native challengers instead: Lemonade at 213, Root Insurance at 165, against State Farm's 172 and Progressive's 114.

The pattern repeats outside insurance. Sephora, Samsung, Whirlpool, and GE Appliances were displaced in their categories; Medtronic, GoodRx, and 23andMe underperformed their SEO strength in health; even Trustpilot — a site whose entire product is third-party opinion — made the underrepresented list. On the other side, the overperformers cluster where the wider web writes about the brand constantly: education had the highest overperformer rate at 7%, led by Stanford, MIT, Khan Academy, and LinkedIn Learning.

Fractl's explanation is the one the citation data has been pointing at all year: models learn brands from what the web repeatedly says about them, not from what brands say about themselves. A legacy insurer's authority lives in its own domain — product pages, agent locators, a content hub. A challenger brand's presence lives in comparison articles, Reddit threads, and press coverage, which is the corpus models are trained and grounded on. That is the same asymmetry we found in [where AI citations come from](/blog/where-ai-citations-come-from), where 84% of citations pointed at domains the brand doesn't own. The index shows the training-data version of the same effect: authority you wrote about yourself transfers worst.

## Three models, three different markets

The finding that should change how teams report, though, is not the disappearing incumbents. It is the consistency number. Of more than 8,500 brands referenced across the study, **only about 900 — 11% — were mentioned by all three models.** Twelve percent appeared in two. Seventy-seven percent appeared in exactly one.

At the level of a single brand, the spread is not a rounding difference. Notion was mentioned roughly 17 times more often by Claude than by Gemini; Whoop nearly 4 times more. The models also lean by category — Claude toward SaaS and insurance, Gemini toward travel and healthcare, with ChatGPT showing the most overlap with the other two.

The operational consequence: an [AI share of voice](/blog/ai-share-of-voice) number averaged across engines describes a market that doesn't exist. A brand can dominate its category on one model, be absent on another, and report a mediocre blended score that motivates exactly the wrong work. This is the cross-sectional twin of what our [citation volatility](/blog/citation-volatility) data shows over time — the engines disagree with each other at any moment as much as each engine disagrees with itself across months — and it is why [prompt tracking](/blog/ai-prompt-tracking) has to keep engines separate to mean anything.

## What this does not show

Four discounts, and the first one changes what the study is.

**This is a study of model APIs, not AI search products.** GPT-4o, Gemini 2.5 Flash, and Claude Sonnet 4.6 were queried directly, and neither write-up says whether web grounding was enabled. Without grounding, an answer comes from parametric recall — what the model memorized in training — not from live retrieval. Those are different systems that routinely disagree, which is the same product-versus-API gap that makes [approximating ChatGPT with a model API](/blog/best-llm-scraping-apis) unreliable. "Vanishing from AI search" is the headline; "absent from model memory" is the measurement. Both matter, but a brand invisible to raw GPT-4o may still be cited constantly by ChatGPT with browsing, and vice versa.

**One of the three models is aging.** GPT-4o is not what ChatGPT serves in 2026, and the study's own cross-model spread is the argument for why that matters: if 77% of brands appear in only one model, results from a superseded model describe a market that is already being replaced.

**It is vendor-authored, with no raw data.** Both write-ups are contributed articles by Fractl's co-founder, and Fractl sells the remedy its conclusion recommends — earned third-party coverage. The prompt list, the brand-extraction method, and the underlying counts are not published, so nothing here is independently checkable the way the [rank-overlap studies](/blog/rankings-and-ai-citations) at least partially are. The method is sound and the direction matches independent data; hold the specific counts loosely.

**A mention is not a recommendation, and neither is a citation.** The index counts brand references in model output. Whether the model recommended the brand, named it neutrally, or mentioned it as the option to avoid is not broken out — and as the [ghost citations](/blog/ghost-citations) data shows, presence and endorsement come apart in AI answers routinely.

## What to do with it

1. **Build the two-axis view for your own brand.** Domain rating and organic traffic on one axis, AI mentions per model on the other. Aligned, underrepresented, or overperforming — the classification takes an afternoon and tells you whether your rank reports are still a usable proxy.
2. **Report per model, permanently.** The 17x Notion gap is the argument. If your dashboard averages engines, it is hiding the only structure this dataset found.
3. **Test recall and retrieval separately.** Ask the raw model who leads your category, then ask the search-grounded product. A brand missing from both has a corpus problem; missing from recall only, a retrieval opportunity; missing from retrieval only, a content problem. The fixes don't overlap.
4. **If you're an incumbent, audit where your authority lives.** If the answer is "mostly our own domain," you are structurally shaped like the brands on the underrepresented list. Third-party comparisons, community presence, and editorial coverage are what models internalize.
5. **Watch model transitions like algorithm updates.** The index is a snapshot of three specific model versions. When an engine swaps its underlying model, your presence can move without anything on the web changing — re-baseline rather than assuming continuity.

## Where this leaves the SEO-transfers assumption

The reassuring reading of this index is that for nine brands in ten, nothing new is required: the authority work SEO already rewards carries into AI visibility. The uncomfortable reading is that you cannot know which group you're in from SEO data alone, because the whole finding is that the proxy fails silently — a brand can watch healthy rank reports while three models replace it with a challenger in the answers buyers actually read. The only way to know is to [measure the answers directly](/blog/track-brand-ai-search), per model, against the competitors the models actually name — which is [competitor analysis](/blog/ai-competitor-analysis), not rank tracking.
