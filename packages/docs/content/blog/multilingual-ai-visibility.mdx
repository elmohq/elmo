**Most AI visibility programs track an English prompt set and quietly assume the results generalize to every market the brand sells in. Three datasets from the past six months — one academic, two at billion and multi-million citation scale — say they don't.** Change the language of the prompt and, for most commercially important languages, you change the citation pool itself: different pages, different platforms, and a competitor set your English tracking has never seen.

**Key takeaways**

- An [August 2026 preprint](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.00319) auditing 15,942 citations across ChatGPT, Perplexity, and Google AI Overview found non-English queries surfaced fewer citations (9.2 per response versus 12.3 in English) and were routed to resources in the query's language at sharply lower rates.
- The engines diverge more outside English than in it. Perplexity held a stable 8.6–10.2 citations per response across all seven languages tested; ChatGPT collapsed from 17.5 sources in English to 4.3 in Twi; Google AI Overview returned zero citations for 30% of Hindi responses.
- At commercial scale the direction holds but the split flips: [Temso's analysis of 7.06 million citations](https://www.temso.ai/data/-Lost-in-Translation-How-AI-Models-Handle-Local-Language-Sources) across seven European languages found engines citing *local-language* pages most of the time — 85.4% on Google AI Overviews, down to 51.7% on Grok. For high-resource languages, the citation pool is a separate, native-language web.
- Language beats geography. [Profound's March 2026 study](https://www.tryprofound.com/blog/how-query-language-reshapes-ai-citations) of 3.25 billion citations across 14 countries found citation composition tracked the prompt language, not the country: Spanish queries behaved almost identically in Mexico and Spain on the same engine.
- The two failure modes are opposites, and both are invisible to English-only tracking. High-resource languages get a localized pool where your English citations don't exist; low-resource languages get English fallback with thinner, sparser answers.
- The decision rule: track a separate native-language prompt set for every high-resource language your buyers prompt in. Markets that share a language can share a set.

## The academic audit: fewer citations, worse routing

The spine of the multilingual evidence is a [preprint posted to arXiv on 31 August 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.00319), "Sources of Truth," which audited what ChatGPT, Perplexity, and Google AI Overview actually cite. The English core ran 20 mental health questions through each platform's free tier, five repetitions each, in two prompt conditions — 600 responses, 10,972 citations. A subset of three questions was then translated into six more languages chosen across resource tiers: Spanish and Japanese (high-resource), Ukrainian and Hindi (medium), Nepali and Twi (low) — another 540 responses and 4,970 citations.

Two findings matter for anyone responsible for visibility outside English.

**Non-English answers are thinner.** Mean citations per response fell from 12.3 in English to 9.2 across the other six languages, ranging from 11.2 for Spanish down to 7.1 for Twi. The degradation was engine-specific: on the comparison questions, ChatGPT averaged 17.5 sources in English against 4.3 in Twi, and returned no citations at all in 33% of Twi responses — and, surprisingly, in 23% of Japanese ones, high-resource or not. Google AI Overview came back citation-free for 30% of Hindi responses. Perplexity was the outlier in consistency, holding 8.6–10.2 citations per response across every language tested.

**Routing to the query's language is uneven and tier-dependent.** The paper measured whether citations pointed at resources a speaker of the query language could use:

| Language | Resource tier | Citations on local ccTLDs | Language-appropriate pages (any domain) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Japanese | High | 77.3% | 78.4% |
| Spanish | High | 11.3% | 46.2% |
| Ukrainian | Medium | 31.1% | 42.6% |
| Twi | Low | 2.5% | 37.6% |
| Hindi | Medium | 11.8% | 35.9% |
| Nepali | Low | 3.9% | 24.9% |

High-resource languages received 38.0% of citations on national domains; low-resource languages got 3.3%. The Spanish and Twi rows also show why the ccTLD column alone misleads: counting native-language pages on non-local domains lifted Spanish from 11.3% to 46.2%, because so much Spanish-language content lives on `.com` and `.org`. Measured either way, though, a large share of non-English answers was being assembled from sources not in the asker's language at all.

## The commercial corpora agree on the direction — and flip the split

One academic audit in one domain would be suggestive. Two first-party citation corpora at vastly larger scale point the same direction while filling in the part the paper couldn't cover: what happens in the languages where brands actually spend money.

Temso matched cited URLs to detected page language across 7,058,891 citations from roughly 350,000 AI responses in seven European languages, 12 countries, and 47 industry verticals in early 2026:

| Engine | Local-language citations | English citations | Other |
|---|---|---|---|
| Google AI Overviews | 85.4% | 8.7% | 5.9% |
| Microsoft Copilot | 76.7% | 15.4% | 7.9% |
| ChatGPT | 70.2% | 23.7% | 6.1% |
| Grok | 51.7% | 39.6% | 8.7% |

For the major European languages, in other words, the engines mostly *do* localize — which is the more uncomfortable finding for an English-only tracking program, not the less. A German buyer's AI Overview is built overwhelmingly from German-language pages. Your English citations are not merely diluted in that pool; they are absent from it. Temso also found the localization gap runs by language family — Dutch and Swedish localized worst, to the point that Grok answered Dutch prompts with more English pages (53.5%) than Dutch ones (38.3%) — and by vertical, from 76.9% local-language in K-12 education down to 35.5% in hotels and hospitality.

Profound's March 2026 analysis adds the finding that makes this operationally simpler: across 3.25 billion citations, 7 engines, and 14 countries, citation composition followed the *language*, not the country. Spanish queries produced near-identical patterns in Mexico and Spain on the same engine. Within Google AI Overviews' social citations, YouTube carried 38% of the social slice for English but 65% for Portuguese; TikTok went from 3.1% in English to 15.9% in Spanish; Instagram from 4.6% in English to 29% in Arabic. Meanwhile ChatGPT's social citations were 51–76% Reddit in every market studied — platform habit overriding localization. The surfaces that carry an answer are language-specific and engine-specific at once.

## Reconciling 46% with 85%

Set the paper's Spanish figure (46.2% language-appropriate) next to Temso's Spanish figure (83–84% local-language on the top engines) and you have an apparent contradiction worth resolving rather than averaging.

Three things separate the measurements. First, **domain**: the audit asked mental health questions, a topic where the authoritative sources — WHO, government health agencies, academic journals — publish predominantly in English, pulling every language's citations toward English institutions. Temso's 47 verticals are full of topics where local businesses, local publishers, and local review sites are the natural sources. Second, **scale**: the paper's non-English evidence is three questions per language, 90 responses each — a careful probe, not a census. Temso's is seven million citations. Third, **tier coverage**: Temso tested only high-resource European languages, exactly the segment where localization works; the paper's distinctive contribution is showing what happens below that tier, where localization quietly fails and English fallback takes over.

The reconciliation is one sentence: **how localized an AI answer's citation pool is depends on the language's resource tier and on whether local publishers cover the topic — so the question "does my English visibility carry over?" has opposite answers in Munich and Kathmandu.** In high-resource markets you are invisible in a pool you don't track; in low-resource markets you may be inherited from the English web into answers that are thinner and less useful to the buyer reading them.

## What this means for a tracking program

The practical consequence is that "AI visibility" is not one number for an international brand, and the standard setup — an English prompt set, [tracked across engines](/blog/track-brand-ai-search), reported as a single [share of voice](/blog/ai-share-of-voice) — measures exactly one of your markets.

Three specifics follow from the data rather than from general caution:

1. **Prompt sets should be per language, not per country.** Profound's Mexico–Spain result means a Spanish set covers both markets; a German set covers Germany and Austria. This cuts the tracking cost of going multilingual considerably.
2. **Engine weighting should change per language.** An engine that returns no citations for 30% of responses in a language cannot cite you there, and an engine that answers Dutch prompts from English pages is one where your English footprint still matters. The engine mix worth paying for in each language is an empirical question your first translated prompt run answers.
3. **The competitor set is language-specific.** If 85% of the German citation pool is German-language pages, the brands and publishers winning those citations are substantially domestic. A share-of-voice report built on English prompts will not contain them. This is the same lesson as [the citation source mix](/blog/where-ai-citations-come-from) generally — the pool you're competing in is narrower and more specific than any global headline — applied along a new axis.

The audit itself is cheap relative to what it decides. Translate your top ten prompts properly (not machine-translated keyword strings — buyers prompt in natural language), run them across the engines your buyers use in each language, and read what comes back: citation counts, cited-page language, and who is named. That one afternoon tells you which markets are separate surfaces and which are riding your English work.

## The limits worth stating

The academic audit is a preprint, its multilingual arm is small, and its domain — mental health — is about as institutionally sourced as topics get; commercial categories will localize more, as Temso's data shows. Its ccTLD measure undercounts language-appropriateness, which the authors themselves demonstrate by publishing both columns. Temso and Profound are both AI visibility vendors measuring the problem their products address, and Temso's headline numbers cover only high-resource European languages on four engines — nothing there speaks to Hindi, Ukrainian, or any low-resource market. Profound's most striking per-language numbers describe the social slice of citations, not the whole pool. And all three datasets are snapshots of systems that [change under your feet](/blog/citation-volatility): engine localization behavior in early 2026 is a moving baseline, which is an argument for re-running the check, not for skipping it.

None of those caveats touch the core finding, because all three datasets — different methods, different incentives, different language sets — agree on it: the prompt's language selects the citation pool, and English-only tracking sees exactly one pool.
