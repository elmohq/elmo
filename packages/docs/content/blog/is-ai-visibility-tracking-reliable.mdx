**Two practitioner pieces published within a day of each other in August 2026 gave marketers opposite advice about the same problem.** One, from AgencyAnalytics CMO Danielle Brown in [Search Engine Journal](https://www.searchenginejournal.com/organic-traffic-dropped-what-to-tell-clients-the-ai-visibility-metrics-to-report-instead-spa/584388/), tells agencies exactly which AI visibility metrics to report when a client asks why organic traffic fell. The other, from Browser Media's Victoria Spall, is titled [Why measuring AI visibility is (mostly) BS](https://browsermedia.agency/blog/why-measuring-ai-visibility-is-mostly-bs/) and argues the whole category is dashboards over sand.

Both are worth taking seriously, and the interesting part is that the critique lands on some of the advice published beside it. The objections are mostly correct about the instrument. They are wrong about the conclusion.

**Key takeaways**

- The demand is real and so is the pressure behind it: in AgencyAnalytics' [2026 Marketing Agency Benchmarks Report](https://agencyanalytics.com/agency-benchmarks-2026), 42% of agencies field questions about dropped organic traffic, 64% name AI Overviews their top industry concern, and 66% report client demand for answer engine optimization — the #1 new service request of the year.
- Of six common objections to prompt-based measurement, three hold outright, two hold partially, and one is weaker than stated.
- "Nobody has real query data" is the weak one. **Clickstream panels** supply real prompts at scale — Semrush's 2026 index analysed 126 million US AI search prompts — and Microsoft's own citation logs are engine-side observed data.
- Instability is the objection that holds hardest, and it is measurable rather than fatal. Our [citation volatility study](/blog/citation-volatility) found the domains cited for a prompt turning over roughly 60–70% day to day.
- Which is why the mainstream advice — 20 to 30 prompts, run once a month — fails the [IAB's measurement bar](/blog/iab-ai-visibility-metrics) published a week earlier, where under 50 queries is "exploratory" and a single run is not a rate.
- Personalization is unfixable and under-discussed. ChatGPT memory can reference a user's past chats, so logged-out measurement is a convention for comparability, not a picture of what buyers see.
- The defensible output of all this is not a score. It is a rate, over many runs, with a stated **noise floor** — and a decision that turns on a margin wider than that floor.

## The six objections, rated

Spall's piece is the most complete list of the standard criticisms in one place, so it makes a useful checklist. Here is each one with a verdict.

| Objection | Verdict | Why |
|---|---|---|
| No access to real user queries | **Overstated** | Panels supply real prompts; engine logs are the part that stays closed |
| Auto-generated prompt sets misrepresent the business | **Holds** | The input determines everything downstream, and defaults are generic |
| The same question returns different answers | **Holds** | Measurable, and the reason cadence and sample size are not optional |
| Mentions cannot be attributed to conversions | **Holds partially** | Partial downstream data exists; the full path does not |
| Personalization means no two users see the same answer | **Holds** | Documented product behaviour, no available workaround |
| High cost for unproven return | **Contingent** | A pricing objection, not a methodological one |

### 1. "Nobody has the query logs" — overstated

The strong form of this claim is true and important: no external tool can see what users actually asked ChatGPT, Gemini, or Perplexity, or what those engines actually answered. That is a closed system, and every prompt-based figure in circulation is a **synthetic prompt set** standing in for behaviour nobody outside the engine can observe.

The weaker form — that no real prompt data exists at all — does not survive contact with the market. Semrush [sources its AI visibility data](https://www.semrush.com/kb/1607-semrush-ai-visibility-data) from AI search clickstream panels alongside Google's keyword dataset, and its [2026 AI Visibility Index](https://www.semrush.com/news/463141-semrush-releases-expanded-2026-ai-visibility-index-analyzing-126-million-ai-search-prompts/) analysed 126 million US AI search prompts from January to April 2026 across ChatGPT, Gemini, Google AI Mode, and AI Overviews. That is real user behaviour, sampled through a panel rather than invented.

There is also genuine engine-side data, just not from the engines most people care about. [Microsoft Clarity's Citation dashboard](/blog/microsoft-clarity-ai-citations) reports the grounding queries Microsoft's own retrieval used before generating answers, which is a log rather than a sample. It covers one ecosystem, and it exists.

So the honest statement is narrower than the objection: prompt sets are a sample, real prompt data can inform which prompts you sample, and the answer distribution stays invisible.

### 2. Auto-generated prompt sets — holds

This is the objection with the shortest path to a fix and the one most often skipped. A tool that generates your prompt set from a domain and a category will produce plausible-looking queries about a business it does not understand, and every number after that inherits the error. A set weighted toward your brand name measures the ceiling — the same trap Microsoft's [branded and non-branded split](/blog/microsoft-clarity-ai-citations) was built to expose.

Write the set from things buyers actually said: sales call recordings, support tickets, the questions your best-converting pages answer, and the category questions asked by people who have not heard of you. Then check the mix covers broad recommendation prompts, head-to-head comparisons, and narrow buying-intent queries, because those three pull from different pools.

### 3. Instability — holds, and it is the load-bearing one

"Ask the same model the same question twice, and you can get different citations" is Spall's phrasing, and it understates the size of the effect. Across 28 prompts tracked daily on ChatGPT and Google AI Mode for 42 days, we found the set of domains cited for a given prompt turned over by roughly **60–70% from one day to the next**. Underneath the churn there was a stable core — on Google AI Mode a small group of domains earned about 56% of citations every day — but the surface layer moves constantly.

This does not make measurement impossible. It makes single readings worthless, which is a different claim. Rainfall varies enormously day to day and nobody concludes that rain gauges are BS; they report monthly totals and compare years. The equivalent discipline here is a fixed set, repeated runs per period, and a published noise floor.

### 4. Attribution — holds partially

No tool can tell you that a mention in an AI answer produced a customer. The path is often invisible: the assistant answers, the user never clicks, and the conversion arrives weeks later through a branded search. AgencyAnalytics' benchmark data puts numbers on how much this hurts, with 48% of agencies struggling to track users who discover brands through AI tools, against 89% saying their clients care about outcomes rather than traffic.

Partial downstream data does exist. Clarity reports AI referral traffic as a share of sessions. Search Console counts AI Mode activity. And the traffic that does arrive is unusually good — [AI referrals convert better than search while being a fraction of a percent of visits](/blog/ai-referral-traffic-conversion). What none of it does is close the loop between a specific mention and a specific sale, and anyone promising otherwise is selling.

### 5. Personalization — holds, with no workaround

This is the objection that deserves more attention than it gets. ChatGPT's [memory feature](https://openai.com/index/memory-and-new-controls-for-chatgpt/) can reference a user's past chats when answering, which means the retrieval and the recommendation both shift with accumulated context. Two buyers with identical questions and different histories get different brands, different orderings, and different sources.

Measuring logged out, in a private session, with fixed wording — the standard advice, and correct as far as it goes — removes personalization from your readings. That buys comparability between your own runs. It does not recover what any individual buyer saw, and nothing available does. An AI visibility figure is therefore a measurement of the unpersonalized baseline, which is a real and useful thing to track as long as nobody in the room believes it is a survey of your customers' screens.

### 6. Cost — contingent

Tracking a serious prompt set across several engines on a real cadence costs money every month, and the return is not proven in the way a paid-search dashboard's is. That is a fair objection to a purchase, not to a method. It is also the one objection with a structural answer: the sampling can run on infrastructure you own, which turns most of a subscription into API usage you control and makes the method inspectable rather than proprietary. That is the case for the [free and open-source end of the category](/blog/free-ai-visibility-tools).

## The playbook published beside the critique has the same flaw

Brown's SEJ piece recommends four metrics — visibility, position, citations, sentiment — which map cleanly onto the IAB's [4 P's](/blog/iab-ai-visibility-metrics) and are the right four things to report. The method attached to them is where it gets interesting: "Write 20 to 30 prompts real buyers would use, and repeat the set per location if the client serves multiple markets. Run them monthly, logged out of the AI platform or in a private session, using the same wording each time."

Three of those instructions are exactly right. Real buyer prompts, consistent wording, and logged-out sessions are the fundamentals. Two of them collide with what was published a week earlier and with the volatility data.

| The advice | The constraint it meets |
|---|---|
| 20 to 30 prompts | The IAB's guidance treats under 50 queries as "exploratory" — below even directional — because fewer than 50 cannot characterize a category |
| Run them monthly | One run is one observation. With 60–70% day-to-day churn in cited domains, a single monthly reading cannot separate a trend from a Tuesday |

The arithmetic is unforgiving on the first point. In a 25-prompt set, one prompt flipping from mentioned to not mentioned moves your visibility rate by four points. A client presentation that treats a four-point month-over-month change as progress is presenting one prompt's mood.

None of this makes the advice bad — it is far better than the reporting it replaces, and it is aimed at agencies who need something defensible on Monday. But it is a directional method being handed to people whose clients will read it as decision-grade, which is precisely the confusion the IAB tiers were written to prevent. The fix is cheap: more prompts, more runs per period, and a stated margin.

## What the first-party alternative can and cannot do

Spall's recommendation is to lean on data you own: Google Search Console and Bing Webmaster Tools, including the odd conversational fragments that show up as queries. That instinct is right, and it has a real basis. AI Mode activity is counted in Search Console's Performance report under the Web search type, and Google's documentation on [AI features](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/appearance/ai-features) explains that a follow-up question inside AI Mode is treated as a new query, with its impressions, position, and clicks attributed there.

The ceiling arrives quickly. The [generative AI performance report](https://support.google.com/webmasters/answer/16984139) carries no query-level data, so the AI-specific view and the query view are separate things — a gap [Search Engine Roundtable documented](https://www.seroundtable.com/google-search-console-ai-mode-queries-41821.html) when the reports shipped. And no first-party report, from Google or Microsoft, can tell you the three things that make AI answers a brand problem rather than a traffic problem: whether the answer named you, whether it described you accurately, and which competitor it recommended instead. Log data reports retrieval. It cannot report [what was said about you](/blog/ai-brand-sentiment), and it will not report the 61.7% of brand appearances that turn out to be [citations with no brand mention](/blog/ghost-citations).

First-party and sampled data are complements, and the strongest version of Spall's argument is really an argument for using both rather than for using neither.

## What a defensible number looks like

Strip out the overstatement on both sides and a workable standard is left.

1. **A prompt set built from observed buyer language**, not generated from a domain, and large enough that one prompt cannot move the headline.
2. **Repeated runs per reporting period**, on fixed wording and fixed session conditions, so the figure is a rate rather than a reading.
3. **A published noise floor**, found by running the set twice under identical conditions, so movements can be sorted into signal and weather.
4. **Stated engine coverage**, because a number from one engine describes one engine.
5. **Both kinds of data**: sampled prompts for what the answers say, first-party logs for what retrieval actually did.
6. **Decisions sized to the instrument.** Whether you appear at all on category questions, whether a rival is consistently named ahead of you, and whether an answer is factually wrong about your pricing are large signals that survive the noise. Ranking a dashboard to a decimal place is not.

That standard rules out most of what is currently reported as AI visibility, which is the fair part of the critique. It does not rule out measurement.

## The honest summary

The critics are right that the category oversells precision, that generated prompt sets are frequently junk, and that personalization puts a permanent ceiling on what any of this can claim. The vendors are right that a brand being recommended, mis-described, or omitted in the answers buyers now rely on is a thing worth knowing about, and that guessing is not the superior alternative.

What is actually broken is the format. A single number with no prompt count, no cadence, no engine list, and no error bar is not a measurement, and that is true whether it comes from a $2,000-a-month platform or a spreadsheet. Report the rate, the range, and the method, and the objections above mostly turn into footnotes.
