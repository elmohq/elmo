**You can win the auction and still lose the page.** On a Google results page carrying an AI answer, two systems are deciding who gets named: an ad auction you can pay into, and a retrieval process you cannot. They run independently, they sit inches apart, and in mid-2026 there is now a large-sample measurement of how rarely they agree. SE Ranking checked 50,032 commercial keywords and found that the advertiser's own domain appeared among the sources cited in the AI answer 11.53% of the time.

**Key takeaways**

- In [SE Ranking's July 2026 study](https://seranking.com/blog/google-ai-mode-ads/), only 11.53% of advertiser domains appeared among AI Mode's cited sources for keywords they were advertising on. At the exact URL level, 1.95%.
- The authors' own summary is unusually blunt: "the ad slot is a paid-media decision; being cited is an authority-and-content decision, and neither one moves the other."
- Ads showed on 29.45% of the tested commercial keywords — close to one in three, though every keyword in the sample was pre-selected to trigger an ad on ordinary Search.
- Roughly 85% of advertisers did not appear in organic results for the keywords they were bidding on, so most paid presence sits on queries where the brand has no earned presence to convert into a citation.
- The contradiction is visible to users, not just to analysts. [Search Engine Land documented](https://searchengineland.com/when-ai-overviews-contradict-paid-search-ads-484323) a plumbing advertiser holding the top paid slot while the AI Overview named two other companies as the best choice.
- Google's advertiser docs describe where ads sit relative to an AI Overview — above, below, and within it — and say nothing about the answer's sources, because that is a different system.
- Corroborating from the other direction: paid and advertorial content was 0.3% of the 25 million citations in the [largest published source-mix study](/blog/where-ai-citations-come-from).

## Two systems, one page

Google's [advertiser documentation](https://support.google.com/google-ads/answer/16297775?hl=en) is precise about geography and format. Ads are "eligible to show above or below the AI Overview" across all 200-plus markets where AI Overviews run, and can also appear "within the AI Overviews on Search" — that inner placement being English-only, on mobile and desktop, in thirteen named countries. Text and Shopping ads from existing Search, Shopping, and Performance Max campaigns are eligible. There is one documented category carve-out: "Currently, we don't show ads in AI Overviews for sensitive verticals like adult, alcohol, gambling, finance, healthcare, politics, and more."

What the documentation does not describe is any relationship between buying that slot and being named in the answer next to it. That is not an oversight. Placement is sold; source selection is not, and the pages describing one have no reason to describe the other.

The consequence is a page with two independent verdicts on it. The ad says *choose us, here is a promotion and a 4.9-star average*. The answer above or below it says *the best option is these other companies*. Both were generated by Google, seconds apart, for the same query.

## The overlap, measured

SE Ranking's study, published on 14 July 2026 by Yevheniia Khromova and Svitlana Tomko, took 50,032 commercial keywords across 20 niches — roughly 2,500 each — all selected because they trigger a text ad, and collected US AI Mode results on 30 June 2026.

| Measure | Result |
|---|---|
| Keywords returning a text ad in AI Mode | 29.45% (14,733) |
| Ad-triggering keywords showing two ads together | 71.1% |
| Advertiser **domain** among the answer's cited sources | 11.53% |
| Advertised **URL** among the answer's cited sources | 1.95% |
| Advertised URL ranking organically for the same query | 2.32% |
| Advertiser domain ranking organically for the same query | 15.35% |

Read the last four rows together and the picture is of two near-disjoint sets. Nine in ten advertisers were absent from the sources of the answer running beside their ad. The exact page they were paying to send traffic to was cited on fewer than one query in fifty.

The organic rows explain a good part of why. About 85% of advertisers did not rank organically for the keywords they were bidding on, which is a long-standing property of paid search rather than anything new: bidding is precisely how brands appear on queries they have not earned. But it means most paid presence sits on queries where the brand has no retrievable asset for the answer to draw on. The ad is not competing with the citation. It is standing in for one that was never there.

Note the surface, because it changes what the number is about. This is **AI Mode**, Google's conversational surface, not AI Overviews. The two behave differently enough that [published rank–citation overlap estimates for them differ by more than fifty points](/blog/rankings-and-ai-citations) in the same month, and their ad availability differs too. An ad–citation overlap figure carries the surface it was measured on, the same way every other number in this field does.

## Where the ads are is not where the citations are

Two of the study's secondary breakdowns are more interesting than the headline, because they show the ad system responding to commercial pressure in exactly the places the citation system does not.

The first is price. Ad presence in AI Mode climbed with cost per click: 24.33% on keywords under $2, 32.45% between $2 and $10, and 53.56% at $10 and above. CPC was the strongest single predictor SE Ranking tested. Nothing in any published citation research shows sources being selected on anything resembling that gradient.

The second is category. Pets carried ads on 72.38% of tested keywords; healthcare on 2.64%. The healthcare floor lines up with Google's own documented exclusion of sensitive verticals from in-overview ads, and it produces a neat inversion. Healthcare is the category where [BrightEdge measured the tightest link between ranking and citation](/blog/rankings-and-ai-citations) — 75.3% of AI Overview citations coming from organically ranking pages — and it is close to the category where paid placement is least available. The surfaces where money can buy you onto the page and the surfaces where earned authority decides the answer are, if anything, pulling apart.

## What a user actually sees

The abstraction becomes concrete in the examples Sarah Stemen published in [Search Engine Land](https://searchengineland.com/when-ai-overviews-contradict-paid-search-ads-484323) on 5 August 2026.

On "what is the best plumber for a broken pipe," a plumbing company held the top paid position with an $89 leak-detection offer, a 4.9-star rating across 20,000-plus reviews, 24/7 availability, a video carousel, and location extensions — a maximally built-out ad. The AI Overview on the same page answered that the best plumber "is a local 24/7 emergency plumbing company like Roto-Rooter Plumbing & Water Cleanup or Amanda Plumbing." The advertiser was not mentioned.

On "sweatshirts for anxiety," the AI Overview named three brands at three price points. The company bidding hardest at the top of the page was not among them. The detail worth keeping is what happened next: rephrased as "what is an anxiety sweatshirt," that same brand appeared. Its absence was not a verdict on quality. It was a categorisation outcome — the engine had filed the brand under a definition rather than a recommendation — and it flipped on a change of wording the buyer made unconsciously.

Two screenshots are not a dataset, and Stemen presents them as illustrations rather than measurement. Their value is showing what the 11.53% looks like at the level of one person, one query, one page: a paid endorsement and an editorial-sounding rejection, rendered together, both by Google.

## What this does not prove

The study measures overlap, not effect, and the distinction matters as much here as it does for [rank and citation](/blog/rankings-and-ai-citations).

An 11.53% overlap is consistent with "buying ads does nothing for citation." It is also consistent with "advertisers are systematically the brands least likely to be cited anyway," which the organic figures actively suggest — a brand that bids on a query it does not rank for is, by construction, a brand without a retrievable asset there. Nobody has compared matched advertisers against non-advertisers on the same queries, which is the design that would separate those two stories. The honest claim is independence, not a proven absence of mechanism.

The figure is also a single day's snapshot of one surface in one market, taken while ad formats inside AI answers are still expanding. SE Ranking flags this itself, noting that ad behaviour "may change as Google expands AI-specific formats." Treat 29.45% as a reading with a date on it.

What survives all of that is the direction, and it is corroborated from an unrelated angle. In Muck Rack's analysis of more than 25 million cited links, paid and advertorial content accounted for 0.3% of citations — a rounding error in [where AI citations come from](/blog/where-ai-citations-come-from). Two studies with different methods, different engines, and different questions land on the same shape: paid signals are not what these systems retrieve on.

## What to do with it

The practical move is not to change your bids. It is to stop letting two dashboards describe the same page without ever meeting.

1. **Use your highest-spend commercial queries as a prompt set.** They are already prioritised by money, and you already have performance data to join against.
2. **Record every cited domain and every brand named,** including the runs where you do not appear. Whether a rival was recommended above your paid slot is the finding, and self-focused tracking cannot see it.
3. **Report domain-level and URL-level overlap separately.** The published gap between them is roughly six-fold; collapsing them flatters your position.
4. **Keep AI Overviews and AI Mode apart,** as separate engines with separate ad availability and separate retrieval behaviour.
5. **Build the list of queries where you pay and are never cited.** That list is a content and off-site brief. It is not a bidding problem, and raising the bid will not shorten it.
6. **Test rephrasings of your top commercial questions.** Appearing under one framing and vanishing under another tells you how the engine has categorised you, which is fixable in a way that "the engine prefers competitors" is not.

The measurement question underneath all of this is the same one that governs everything on these surfaces: what did the answer actually say, and who did it name. [Elmo](https://www.elmohq.com/) is an open-source, self-hosted AI visibility platform that runs your prompt sets across ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini, Perplexity, and Google's AI surfaces on a schedule, recording every mention, every cited URL, and every competitor named beside you. Because the data is yours to query directly, joining it to your ad spend by keyword is a join, not an export request.

## The reframe

Paid search has always offered a straightforward bargain: the top of the page is for sale, and everyone knows it, which is why it is labelled. That bargain still holds. What changed is that the most-read part of the page is no longer for sale, and it now issues conclusions rather than listing options.

So a category of spend that used to buy proximity to the decision now buys proximity to a verdict it cannot influence. The budget line that moves that verdict is a different one entirely — the [citations](/blog/ai-citations) your content and your off-site presence earn, on the third-party domains that carry most of them.
