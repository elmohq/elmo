**Roughly half of new articles on the web are AI-generated, and AI engines cite them at about a third of that rate — but the one study claiming engines *prefer* AI content is not wrong either.** Four datasets published between mid-2025 and September 2026 measured the authorship of pages cited in AI answers, and their headlines point in opposite directions: [Graphite](https://graphite.io/five-percent/ai-content-in-search-and-llms) found 82% of ChatGPT and Perplexity citations going to human-written articles, while [Ahrefs](https://ahrefs.com/blog/ai-overviews-cite-ai-generated-content-more-than-human-writing/) headlined that Google's AI Overviews cite AI-generated content *more* than human writing. Read the methodologies instead of the headlines and the contradiction mostly dissolves — into a finding more useful than either summary.

**Key facts**

| Study | Sample | Detector | Headline finding |
|---|---|---|---|
| [Graphite](https://graphite.io/five-percent/ai-content-in-search-and-llms), Oct 2025 | 31,493 keywords, 10 categories; citations sampled June 2025 | Surfer (FPR 4.2%, FNR 0.6%) | 82% of ChatGPT and Perplexity citations classify as human-written; 86% of Google-ranking articles do |
| [Ahrefs](https://ahrefs.com/blog/ai-overviews-cite-ai-generated-content-more-than-human-writing/), Jul 2025 | 1M AI Overview SERPs, top 3 citations each, 500k URLs analyzed | Ahrefs proprietary | Cited pages: 3.6% pure AI, 8.6% pure human, **87.8% mixed** |
| [Pew Research Center](https://www.pewresearch.org/data-labs/2026/08/20/how-much-of-the-internet-is-written-with-ai/), Aug 2026 | ~500,000 English pages, Jan 2021–Jul 2026 | Open Pangram | ~10% of all sampled pages show significant AI authorship; over a third of post-ChatGPT pages do |
| [Brandi AI](https://www.prnewswire.com/news-releases/brandi-ai-research-most-cited-human-written-pages-earn-nearly-six-times-more-ai-citations-than-most-cited-ai-written-pages-831312183.html), Sep 2026 | ~900,000 answers, 4,210 cited pages, Q2 2026, four engines | Proprietary multi-signal | Top 25 human-classified pages averaged 682 citations each vs 119 for the top 25 AI-classified — 5.7x |

**Key takeaways**

- AI-generated articles are about half of new supply — Graphite's [companion study](https://graphite.io/five-percent/more-articles-are-now-created-by-ai-than-humans) of 43,000 Common Crawl URLs found them surpassing human-written articles in November 2024 — but only 18% of ChatGPT and Perplexity citations. Cited at roughly a third of their production share is the honest one-line summary.
- The Ahrefs result that reads as the opposite is a classification artifact worth understanding: purely AI pages are a hair *overrepresented* among AI Overview citations (3.6% vs a 2.5% base rate among new pages), but the story is the 87.8% of citations going to **mixed** human-and-AI pages. Graphite's binary scheme files most of that band under "human."
- Base rates for "how much of the web is AI" span 10% (Pew, all pages) to ~50% (Graphite, new articles) to 74% ([Ahrefs](https://ahrefs.com/blog/what-percentage-of-new-content-is-ai-generated/), new pages containing *any* AI text). None of these is wrong; they answer different denominators, and any preference claim inherits the choice.
- Brandi AI's Q2 2026 tracking — ~900,000 answers across ChatGPT, Google AI Mode, Gemini, and Copilot — found the concentration is at the top: the most-cited human-classified pages took 23% of all citations against 4% for the most-cited AI-classified pages, and in the top 1% of pages the AI-classified share fell below 1%.
- No study controls for page age, and the confound is structural: every page published before late 2022 is human-written by definition, and older pages have had years to accumulate the authority signals engines actually select for. "Engines cite human content" and "engines cite established content" are currently the same observation.
- No dataset shows engines detecting authorship or penalizing AI *assistance*. What underperforms, in every study that can see it, is the page that classifies as predominantly AI — the unedited bulk output.

## How much of the web is AI-written — and why the estimates disagree

Any claim that engines favor or disfavor AI content needs a **base-rate comparison**, and the base rates themselves are contested. Graphite's supply-side study classified 43,000 randomly sampled Common Crawl articles with Surfer's detector (false positive rate 4.2% on pre-ChatGPT text, false negative rate 0.6% on GPT-4o output) and found AI-generated articles overtaking human-written ones in November 2024, then plateauing near half of new supply. Pew Research Center, with roughly 500,000 pages and the Pangram classifier, put significant AI authorship at about 10% of all sampled pages in July 2026.

A 5x disagreement, and both are defensible: Graphite counted **articles** (schema-marked, 100+ words) among **newly published** content; Pew counted **all pages** across five and a half years, most of which predate generative AI, with a different detector drawing the line differently. Ahrefs adds a third denominator — 74% of 900,000 pages newly detected in April 2025 contained *some* AI text, with only 2.5% purely AI. New articles, new pages, and the standing web are three different populations. Every citation-preference number below has to be read against the right one.

## How often do AI answers cite AI-written pages?

Three datasets bear on the citation side, and they triangulate better than their headlines suggest.

**Graphite** ran 100 keywords per category through ChatGPT and Perplexity in June 2025 and classified the cited articles: 82% human-written, 18% AI-generated, for both engines independently. Against an AI share of new-article supply near 50%, that is underrepresentation by roughly a factor of three. The same study found 86% of articles ranking in ordinary Google Search classified as human, with AI-classified articles ranking statistically significantly lower (Wilcoxon signed-rank, p < 1e-6) and taking only 7% of top positions.

**Ahrefs** looked at Google's AI Overviews — 1 million SERPs, the three most visible citations each, 500,000 URLs it could classify — using a three-way scheme. Purely AI pages: 3.6% of citations, against 2.5% of new pages web-wide. Purely human pages: 8.6% of citations, against 25.8% of new pages. Everything else — 87.8% of citations — was mixed. Ahrefs' headline, "AI Overviews cite AI-generated content more than human writing," rests on that first pair: a slight overrepresentation of pure AI, and a large underrepresentation of pure human.

**Brandi AI**, a GEO platform, classified 4,210 pages cited across ~900,000 answers in Q2 2026. Its top 25 human-classified pages averaged 682 citations each; the top 25 AI-classified pages averaged 119 — a 5.7x gap. Among pages still being cited in the study's final month, human-classified pages drew 37% more citations per day (1.80 vs 1.31). Vendor research distributed by press release, with a proprietary detector — the company itself cautions the labels "should not be interpreted as verified authorship" — so it carries less weight than the studies that publish their instruments. But its direction matches Graphite's, on different engines, a year later.

## Why the headlines contradict and the data doesn't

Line the schemes up and the conflict is mostly definitional.

| Classification | Graphite (binary, article-level) | Ahrefs (three-way, page-level) |
|---|---|---|
| Predominantly AI | 18% of assistant citations | 3.6% of AI Overview citations ("pure AI") |
| Mixed human + AI | — folded into the two bins at a >50% threshold | 87.8% of citations |
| Predominantly human | 82% of assistant citations | 8.6% of citations ("pure human") |

Graphite calls an article AI-generated when its detector flags more than half the text, so the enormous mixed band — pages with an AI draft under human editing, or human prose with generated sections — mostly lands in "human." Ahrefs keeps the band separate, and it turns out to be nearly the whole distribution. Restate both studies in each other's vocabulary and they agree on the two claims that matter:

1. **Fully AI-generated articles capture far less citation than their share of production.** Half of new supply; 18% of assistant citations at Graphite's threshold, 3.6% at Ahrefs' strict one.
2. **Purely human prose is no longer the norm among cited pages either.** The modal cited page mixes human and AI writing — which is also what the modal *published* page now looks like.

The residual disagreement — Ahrefs' pure-AI overrepresentation (3.6% vs 2.5%) against everyone else's gaps — is real but small, and sits inside a denominator mismatch: the base rate comes from *new* pages while citations reach the whole standing web. Different engines, too: AI Overviews draw from Google's index in ways [assistants demonstrably don't](/blog/rankings-and-ai-citations).

## The confound every study glosses over

All four datasets share a problem none of them models: the **age confound**. ChatGPT shipped in November 2022. Every page older than that is human-written by construction, and the pages that dominate citation counts are disproportionately established ones — they have had years to earn the links, mentions, and topical standing that drive retrieval. Our own data on [how long citation takes to arrive](/blog/how-long-to-get-cited-by-ai) points the same direction: new pages of *any* authorship start slow.

So when Brandi reports its most-cited human pages out-earning its most-cited AI pages 682 to 119, part of that gap is authorship and part is birthdate, and nothing in the published methodology says how much of each. The clean test — same-age cohorts, same topics, authorship varied — has not been published by anyone. Until it is, "engines prefer human content" and "engines prefer established content, which happens to be human" are observationally identical, and they imply different strategies: the first says avoid AI drafting; the second says the deficit is authority and information, not provenance.

The second glossed-over limit is the instrument itself. Every number above is an **authorship classification**, not a production record. Surfer, Pangram, and two proprietary detectors each draw the human/AI line differently — that is visible in the 10%-vs-50% base-rate spread — and detectors key on stylistic regularities, which means a rigorously edited AI draft reads as human and a formulaic human writer reads as AI. Graphite is candid that its scheme excludes "AI-assisted content with heavy human editing," which it notes "may be an effective strategy." That excluded category is precisely Ahrefs' 87.8%.

## What this does not show

It does not show engines detecting AI content. No engine documents an authorship signal, and the selection pressures we can observe — retrieval, authority, and fit, per the [citation-prediction evidence](/blog/can-content-scores-predict-ai-citations) — explain the gap without one. It does not show that AI drafting *causes* lower citation: these are cross-sectional classifications over pages differing in age, topic, and quality all at once. It does not show Google penalizing AI production; [Google's stated policy](https://developers.google.com/search/blog/2023/02/google-search-and-ai-content) is quality "however it is produced," and its enforcement target is scaled low-value generation. And the assistant-side sample is thin: Graphite's ChatGPT and Perplexity numbers rest on 100 keywords per category in one month of 2025, on engines whose cited sources [turn over heavily](/blog/citation-volatility) day to day.

One boundary worth stating plainly: everything here concerns the authorship of *cited pages*. Whether AI answers describe your brand accurately, and which domains carry your mentions, are separate questions with [their own data](/blog/where-ai-citations-come-from).

## The decision rule

For a team deciding how much AI to put into the content pipeline, the four datasets compress to one rule with two halves:

1. **AI-assisted is where citations already live.** The dominant class of cited page mixes AI and human writing. There is no measured penalty for drafting with a model and editing like you mean it — and no basis in this data for a blanket "no AI" content policy.
2. **Majority-AI, published unedited, is the measured loser.** Half of new supply, a third-of-parity citation rate, 5.7x behind at the top of the distribution. The pages that classify as predominantly AI are competing with an ocean of identical remix, and engines — whatever their mechanism — are not selecting them.

The tiebreaker between the halves is information, not authorship: a page earns citation by containing something the model's training data and the rest of retrieval didn't already have — a number you measured, an experience you had, a position you can defend. AI can draft the container. It cannot supply the contents, because everything it knows is already everywhere. And since the confound cuts both ways, verify the rule on your own pages: classify what gets cited and what doesn't, within the same age band, and let your own join outrank any benchmark — including this one.
