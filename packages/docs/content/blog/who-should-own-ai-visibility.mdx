**Eli Lilly posted a Senior Director of Generative & Answer Engine Optimization this year, your CMO is forwarding articles about brands hiring AI search leads, and someone has asked whether your team needs one too.** The honest answer sits in three datasets that rarely get read together: [Search For Hire's analysis of 328,650 marketing job postings](https://www.searchforhire.com/blog/the-future-of-search-5-experts-and-328650-job-postings-on-what-happens-next/), [Semrush's study of 3,900 SEO listings on Indeed](https://www.semrush.com/blog/seo-job-market-study/), and the [IAB's September 2026 survey of 200+ ad-investment decision-makers](https://www.iab.com/news/iab-raises-2026-u-s-ad-spend-forecast/). Together they say the work is real and growing fast — one in five SEO job ads now asks for it — but that companies are overwhelmingly answering the ownership question by widening the search owner's remit, not by building a parallel team. The dedicated title is the exception, and there is a workload threshold that tells you when you have reached it.

**Key takeaways**

- Demand for the skill is real and compounding: the share of SEO job ads mentioning AI search, GEO, or AEO nearly doubled in six months, from 12.2% in December 2025 to 20.3% in June 2026.
- Demand for the *title* is small: 720 postings in a year named the discipline, against a market where the dominant move is adding AI search to a senior SEO role's requirements.
- The work these roles are hired to do is disproportionately measurement. In the IAB's buyer survey, 86% are changing how they measure media because of AI, and measuring brand visibility and citations inside AI tools is the single most common response, at 48%.
- The advertised 20.5% salary premium for AI search skills is confounded with seniority: new-discipline titles are leadership roles at nearly twice the standard rate, and seniority alone nearly doubles median SEO pay in Semrush's data.
- The datasets disagree on the level (6.3% vs 12.2% at roughly the same moment) because they use different boards, term lists, and denominators — but the only dataset with a time series shows unambiguous growth.
- The practical decision rule: name an explicit owner inside your existing search function first, define the measurement instrument before any hire, and open a dedicated req only when the monitoring and reporting load stops fitting into anyone's existing week.

## Is AEO actually becoming a job?

The largest dataset on the question comes from Search For Hire, a search-marketing recruiting firm that analyzed 328,650 marketing job postings from its Salary Guide job-board database, collected between July 2025 and June 2026. Two of its numbers frame the whole discussion.

The first is the skill line: **20.3% of SEO job ads mentioned AI search, GEO, or AEO skills in June 2026, up from 12.2% in December 2025.** Whatever you think of the discipline's name — we have covered the [AEO-versus-SEO terminology fight](/blog/aeo-vs-seo) before — hiring managers are writing it into every fifth SEO req, and the share nearly doubled in six months.

The second is the title line: **720 postings across twelve months carried the new discipline in the job title itself** — variations like "Director of SEO & AI Search" and "Principal Growth Manager, SEO/AEO." Against hundreds of thousands of SEO postings, that is a rounding error. But the composition is telling: 28% of those new-title roles were leadership positions (Director, Head of, VP, Principal, Lead), versus 15% for standard SEO titles. When a company does decide the work deserves its own title, it hires senior — [Eli Lilly's Senior Director, Generative & Answer Engine Optimization posting](https://careers.lilly.com/us/en/job/R-111696/Senior-Director-Generative-Answer-Engine-Optimization-GEO-AEO) (since closed) being the most visible example.

Semrush's independent study of 3,900 SEO listings on Indeed (November 25, 2025) corroborates the shape from a different board: 59% of all SEO listings were senior leadership roles, general AI appeared in 31% of senior listings, and the specifically AI-search terms — SGE, AEO, AI search — appeared in 6.3% of senior roles versus 3.7% of others. The pattern in both datasets is the same: this work is being attached to senior search scope, not spun out into junior specialist roles.

## Why do the datasets disagree on how big this is?

Put side by side, the two job studies appear to conflict: Semrush finds AI-search terms in 6.3% of senior SEO listings in November 2025; Search For Hire finds 12.2% of SEO ads mentioning the skills a month later. Nearly a two-fold gap at nearly the same moment.

| | Semrush's 6.3% | Search For Hire's 12.2% → 20.3% |
|---|---|---|
| Board | Indeed US only | Salary Guide job-board database |
| Sample | 3,900 listings, single snapshot (Nov 25, 2025) | 328,650 postings, monthly shares (Jul 2025–Jun 2026) |
| Terms counted | "SGE, AEO, AI Search" | AI search, GEO, or AEO skills |
| Denominator | Senior SEO listings (6.3%) vs others (3.7%) | All SEO job ads |
| Extraction | Semantic analysis of listing text | Term tracking across postings |

None of these choices is wrong, and the gap does not need a villain — it is the same instrument problem we documented for [AI visibility scores themselves](/blog/why-ai-visibility-scores-differ): change the corpus, the term list, or the denominator and the level moves. The honest reading is that the level was somewhere in the mid-single to low-double digits at the end of 2025, depending on how you count. What is not in dispute is the direction: the one dataset that measured the same thing monthly shows the share going from 12.2% to 20.3% in six months.

## What are these roles actually hired to do?

The job ads say optimization; the buyer data says measurement. In the IAB's *2026 Outlook Study: September Update* (September 10, 2026, 200+ brand and agency ad-investment decision-makers), **86% of buyers are already changing, or expect to change, how they measure media performance because of conversational AI tools and AI agents** within twelve months. Asked what they are doing about it, the single most common answer — 48% — was **measuring brand visibility and citations directly within AI tools**. Another 45% named comparing AI-driven and traditional customer journeys as their top measurement challenge, and 44% called adapting to AI-driven search a top media-investment challenge outright.

That matters for the hiring question because it describes the actual week of whoever owns this: standing up [prompt tracking across engines](/blog/ai-prompt-tracking), defending a share-of-voice number to a CMO, reconciling it with referral data that [rounds to zero even when it converts well](/blog/ai-referral-traffic-conversion), and reporting on it at a cadence the org trusts. Semrush's skills data points the same direction — project management appears in over 30% of all SEO listings and experimentation in 23.9% of senior ones. The role being assembled across these datasets is a measurement-and-coordination role with optimization attached, not a content-production role. Which [metrics are worth that effort](/blog/iab-ai-visibility-metrics) is its own question, but somebody has to own the answer.

## Is there really an AI-search salary premium?

Search For Hire's headline number says yes: US postings mentioning AI search skills advertised a **median of $117,500 versus $97,500 without — a 20.5% premium.** Before you take that to a compensation review, two caveats the source states but does not dwell on.

First, the salary base is thin: the analysis covers 13,779 postings with advertised salaries, about 40% of the SEO roles in the dataset, and self-selected — companies that advertise pay are not a random sample.

Second, and more important, the premium is not seniority-controlled, and everything else in the data says AI-search mentions skew senior. New-discipline titles are leadership roles at 28% versus 15%; Semrush found the specifically AI-search terms nearly twice as common in senior listings as in others; and Semrush's medians put senior SEO roles at **$130,000 against $71,630** for everyone else. When seniority alone nearly doubles the median, a 20.5% gap between AI-search and non-AI-search postings is at least partly a seniority premium wearing a new name. The defensible claim is narrower: the AI-search work is being placed in senior, senior-paid roles — not that adding "GEO" to a resume is worth $20,000.

## So who should own AI visibility?

The revealed preference across 300,000-plus postings is consistent: **whoever owns organic search, one level up, with the remit made explicit.** Companies are writing AI search into senior SEO reqs twenty times more often than they are creating standalone titles, and when they do create the title, it is a leadership role that consolidates search and AI answers rather than a parallel function.

That default is also the sensible one, for a reason the org-chart debates tend to miss: the inputs overlap almost completely. The pages, the structured data, the third-party coverage, and the review-site presence that drive rankings are the same assets that drive [citations](/blog/ai-citations) — and where they diverge, the divergence is measurable from one tracking setup. What does not work, on the IAB's numbers, is leaving it unowned: 86% of buyers are rebuilding measurement around AI, and an unowned metric is how you end up with two dashboards, two numbers, and [a meeting about why they differ](/blog/why-ai-visibility-scores-differ).

So the first move costs nothing: name the owner, write AI answers into their goals, and give them an instrument — a defined prompt set, engines, and scoring rules — before anyone argues about headcount. How to stand that up is covered in [how to track your brand in AI search](/answer-engine-optimization).

## When does a dedicated hire make sense?

The trigger is workload, and the workload is set by monitoring surface, not ambition. Our own 42-day tracking data gives a sense of scale: the set of domains an engine cited for a single prompt [turned over 55–83% day to day](/blog/citation-volatility), and one broad prompt drew citations from 445 distinct domains over six weeks. Reading that data — separating real movement from churn, per engine, per market — is recurring analytical work, and it grows multiplicatively with engines, markets, and prompt-set size.

Three observable thresholds, any one of which justifies the req:

1. **The reporting is a job in itself.** If the owner spends the better part of a weekly day producing and defending AI visibility numbers — the [client-reporting workload](/blog/ai-visibility-client-reporting), in-house — the "ten percent of an SEO role" framing has already failed.
2. **The coordination is cross-functional.** Most of what AI engines cite about a brand lives on [domains the brand does not own](/blog/where-ai-citations-come-from), so moving the number means brokering work across PR, product marketing, docs, and support. Brokering is a role, not a task.
3. **A competitor is visibly outrunning you.** If your tracked prompts show a rival winning [share of voice](/blog/ai-share-of-voice) quarter over quarter in a category you should own, the cost of the vacancy is no longer hypothetical.

Until one of those trips, the job data's implicit advice holds: make it senior scope, not a new silo — and let the threshold, not the trend pieces, decide when that changes.
