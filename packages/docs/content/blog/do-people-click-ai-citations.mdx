**Mostly no — on other people's AI answers.** In [Pew Research Center's browsing data](https://www.pewresearch.org/short-reads/2025/07/22/google-users-are-less-likely-to-click-on-links-when-an-ai-summary-appears-in-the-results/), Google users clicked a source cited in an AI Overview in about 1% of visits to pages that showed one. But a [randomized experiment at The Washington Post](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.38946) found readers clicking a cited article after 14.3% of AI-answered searches, fourteen times the Google figure. Neither number is wrong. Where the gap comes from tells you what a citation is worth to your brand.

**Key facts**

| | |
|---|---|
| Visits to Google pages with an AI summary that end in a click on a cited source | **~1%** — [Pew Research Center](https://www.pewresearch.org/short-reads/2025/07/22/google-users-are-less-likely-to-click-on-links-when-an-ai-summary-appears-in-the-results/), 900 US adults, March 2025 |
| Visits ending in a conventional result click, with vs without an AI summary | **8% vs 15%** — same data |
| Visits where the user ended the session, with vs without an AI summary | **26% vs 16%** — same data |
| AI-answered searches followed by a click on a cited article, publisher's own search | **14.3%** — [Lee et al., arXiv:2609.38946](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.38946), 37,561 Washington Post readers |
| Change in share of searches leading to an external click, AI Mode-only Google | **−18.8 points** — [Wang et al., arXiv:2608.18352](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.18352), 1,100 participants |
| Change in the same measure with AI Overviews and AI Mode removed | **+8.8 points** — same study |

**Key takeaways**

- On Google, citations in AI Overviews took roughly one click in nine of the clicks that still left the page, by our arithmetic on Pew's figures (1% of visits for citations, 8% for conventional results).
- On a publisher's own AI search, cited articles took nearly a third: 14.3 of the 46.4 article clicks per 100 searches.
- Total clicks fell in both settings. What differed was whether people stopped. AI summaries on Google raised session endings from 16% to 26% of visits; on the Post's site, session endings did not move (21.1% vs 21.3%).
- Google's claim that cited links "get more clicks than if the page had appeared as a traditional web listing" can be true at the same time. It compares a page with itself, not citations with the rest of the results page.
- For brands appearing in third-party AI answers, a citation is an impression. Plan and report it that way, and score the answer text, where your name reaches everyone who reads it.

## Three studies, three different questions

The figures circulating on this topic do not measure the same thing, which is why they look contradictory.

**Pew measured what people do on Google.** The [original analysis](https://www.pewresearch.org/short-reads/2025/07/22/google-users-are-less-likely-to-click-on-links-when-an-ai-summary-appears-in-the-results/) and its [August 2026 paper version](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.04831) use one month of real browsing from 900 US adults in Pew's KnowledgePanel Digital: 68,879 unique Google searches, 12,593 of which produced an AI summary. The unit is a visit to a results page. Clicks on a source inside the summary happened in about 1% of those visits. The data is observational, but the authors report the patterns held after controlling for query attributes and differences between individual panelists.

**The Washington Post experiment measured what readers do next.** Between 1 October and 7 November 2024, readers were randomized by browser cookie at their first on-site search. The 26,639 in the control group saw conventional results. The 10,922 in the treatment group also saw an AI answer citing up to five Post articles above those results. The unit is the next action after a search:

| Next action after a search | Control | AI answer shown |
|---|---|---|
| Clicked a conventional result | 52.9% | 32.1% |
| Clicked a cited article | — | 14.3% |
| Searched again | 10.4% | 21.6% |
| Browsed elsewhere on site | 15.5% | 10.8% |
| Ended the session | 21.1% | 21.3% |

**A preregistered field experiment measured Google's AI features causally.** [Wang, Gleason, Bart, Wilson, and Metaxa](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.18352) recruited US participants and installed a browser extension that rewrote Google Search for seven days in March 2026. Their outcome is external clicks divided by searches. An AI Mode-only Google cut it by 18.8 percentage points (95% CI −22.2 to −15.3). Removing AI Overviews and AI Mode raised it by 8.8 points, even though Google's interface changes mid-study meant only 51.1% of AI Overviews were actually hidden. The study logged outbound clicks overall and did not separate clicks on links cited inside AI answers.

Put together, the three agree on direction: an AI answer reduces clicks to conventional results everywhere it has been measured. They disagree, by an order of magnitude, on how many of those clicks the answer's own citations recover.

## Why a citation on Google is worth a fraction of one on a publisher's site

Four differences between the settings explain most of the gap. The first two are visible in the data; the others are design facts the papers report.

**1. Whether the answer ends the journey.** This is the decisive one. On Google, AI summaries raised the share of visits that ended the session from 16% to 26%. On the Post's site, the share was flat at about 21%, and follow-up searches doubled instead. Post readers treated the answer as a waypoint and kept going. Google users treated it as the destination and left. A citation can only collect clicks from people who are still looking for something to click.

**2. What the citations compete with.** Every source in a Post answer was a Post article, shown to readers who had come to read Post articles. The citation's competition was other links to the same publisher. On Google, a cited source competes with organic results, ads, other features, and other brands' pages, and it is one of several sources in an answer the user may consider complete.

**3. How many citations are visible.** The Post showed up to five cited articles. Pew's paper notes that three sources was the most a desktop Google page highlighted without the user clicking to see more, and that mobile layouts might show none at first.

**4. Who designed the interface, and why.** The Post's authors say outright that effects "may depend on who operates the search interface." A publisher building AI search for its own archive wants the answer to lead into its articles. A general search engine wants the answer to satisfy the query.

That is also how Google's own statement fits. In [May 2024 Google said](https://blog.google/products/search/generative-ai-google-search-may-2024/) "the links included in AI Overviews get more clicks than if the page had appeared as a traditional web listing for that query." In [August 2025](https://blog.google/products/search/ai-search-driving-more-queries-higher-quality-clicks/) it described total organic click volume as "relatively stable year-over-year." Neither post gives a number. The first claim compares a cited page with the same page ranked conventionally, so it can hold even while the results page as a whole sends fewer clicks, as Pew and the field experiment found. Being cited may beat sitting at position seven. It does not bring back the clicks the answer removed.

## What the sources do not tell you

**None of them measures ChatGPT, Perplexity, or Claude citations directly.** Pew covers Google. The Post study covers one publisher's own search. The field experiment logged ChatGPT and Gemini traffic but did not isolate clicks on cited links. Chat assistants put citations in a different place, usually inline or in a sources panel, and their users may be further into a task. Nobody has published a citation click-through rate for them on a comparable sample. Treat Google's 1% as the best-measured reference point, not a law.

**Pew's 1% is an average over every query that triggered a summary.** That data is from March 2025, before AI Mode was widely available, and it is mostly informational: the paper found summaries more likely on longer queries, questions, and full sentences. A buyer comparing two vendors may click through more often than someone asking what a word means. No public dataset splits citation clicks by commercial intent.

**The publisher experiment is one site, in 2024, with an undisclosed model.** The authors do not say what generated the answers or how often treatment searches showed one. The result shows what citations can do when the interface is built to send readers to them. It is not evidence about third-party engines.

**Click-through does not measure exposure.** In the Post experiment, the authors credit AI answers with most of the increase in information readers had in common, delivered "without requiring article clicks." That is the publisher-side version of the point brands keep running into: people read the answer. Whatever it says about you reaches them whether or not they click.

## How to value a citation

For a brand appearing in AI answers it does not control, the evidence supports one rule: **a citation is an impression, and the answer text is the message.**

1. **Report citations next to impressions, not sessions.** On Google's AI Overviews, roughly 1 visit in 100 ends with a click on a cited source. A report that implies citations convert into traffic at organic rates will be wrong, and the people reading it will eventually notice. Our guide to [Search Console and AI visibility](/blog/search-console-ai-visibility) covers what Google's own impression data can and cannot tell you.
2. **Forecast conservatively.** Until you have your own data, model clicks from third-party AI citations at around 1% of answer views, not at the click-through rate of the organic position the page used to hold. If the business case only works at organic rates, it does not work.
3. **Score the work on mentions.** With a quarter of AI-summary visits ending on the spot and many more ending in another search, the brand named in the answer reaches far more people than the link does. Track mention rate, recommendation, and [share of voice](/blog/ai-share-of-voice) alongside citation rate. Engines also cite pages without naming the brand behind them, which is the [ghost citation](/blog/ghost-citations) problem. A citation without a mention can earn you almost nothing.
4. **Measure your own ratio.** Take tracked citation appearances for a page, per engine, and set them against [AI-referred sessions](/blog/ai-referral-traffic-conversion) to that page over the same weeks. The result is noisy and a floor, because in-app browsers and `noreferrer` links strip the referrer. It still beats an industry average.
5. **If you run on-site AI search, cite your own pages.** This is where the Post's numbers apply. When you control the interface and every source is yours, citations captured nearly a third of the article clicks that followed an answer, and doubled follow-up searches kept readers on the site. Follow-up behavior matters on third-party engines too; see [multi-turn AI search](/blog/multi-turn-ai-search) for what single-prompt tracking misses.

The [IAB's AI visibility framework](/blog/iab-ai-visibility-metrics) lists post-citation click-through as a metric under "Persuasion" for a reason: it is the number engines do not hand you. The research so far says not to wait for it. On third-party engines, whether the answer names you matters more than whether anyone clicks the link.
