**Yes, sometimes. And the answer swings by an order of magnitude depending on what the reader came for.** A randomized experiment with 37,561 Washington Post readers found that when an AI answer sat above search results, [readers clicked one of its cited sources after 14.3% of searches](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.38946). On Google, Pew Research Center found people clicked a link inside an AI summary [on 1% of visits](https://www.pewresearch.org/short-reads/2025/07/22/google-users-are-less-likely-to-click-on-links-when-an-ai-summary-appears-in-the-results/). Both studies are sound. They are measuring two different kinds of query, and which kind your prompts are tells you what a citation is worth.

**Key takeaways**

- In the Washington Post experiment, showing an AI answer cut clicks on conventional results from 52.9% to 32.1% of searches, while clicks on cited sources rose from zero to 14.3%. Citations took about **31% of result clicks**.
- On Google, Pew measured 1% of visits ending in a click on an AI-summary link against 8% on a traditional result: roughly **one click in nine**.
- Seer Interactive found brands cited in an AI Overview earned **0.70% organic CTR versus 0.52%** when not cited, both far below the 1.45% on queries with no overview. The citation claws back about a fifth of the loss.
- The disagreement lines up with one variable: whether the cited page is the thing the reader wants (a **destination**) or only supports a fact the answer already gave them (**evidence**).
- AI answers cut clicks per search but increase searching. Washington Post readers searched 29.4% more, and article opens per reader rose 4.9%.
- No dataset shows the citation *causes* the click advantage. Google says AI Overview links get more clicks than ordinary listings, and has never published the numbers.

## Three datasets, three denominators

Every number in circulation about citation clicks is a ratio, and each study divides by something different. Line them up before comparing.

| Source | Setting | Denominator | Cited-source clicks | Conventional-result clicks |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| [Lee, Lee, Lee & Lee (arXiv, 2026)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.38946) | Washington Post archive search, Oct–Nov 2024, randomized | Searches (24,683 treatment) | 14.3% | 32.1% (vs 52.9% in control) |
| [Pew Research Center (2025)](https://www.pewresearch.org/short-reads/2025/07/22/google-users-are-less-likely-to-click-on-links-when-an-ai-summary-appears-in-the-results/) | Google, March 2025, 900 US adults, 68,879 searches | Visits to results pages with an AI summary | 1% | 8% (vs 15% without a summary) |
| [Seer Interactive (2025)](https://www.seerinteractive.com/insights/aio-impact-on-google-ctr-september-2025-update) | Google, Jun 2024–Sep 2025, 3,119 informational queries, 42 organizations | The brand's own organic impressions | n/a | 0.70% when cited, 0.52% when not, 1.45% with no AI Overview |

The first two can be put on a common footing: the share of result clicks that went to a citation. In the Washington Post data that is 14.3 out of 46.4 points, about 31%. In Pew's data it is 1 out of about 9, roughly 11%. Seer's figures answer a different question, how a brand's own listing performs depending on whether it is also cited, and it is the only one of the three built from Search Console data across many sites.

So the spread is not 1% against 14.3%. It is closer to one click in nine against one in three. That is still a large gap, and it has an explanation.

## Why the Washington Post's readers clicked and Google's didn't

The Washington Post study is the strongest causal evidence available on this question. Readers were assigned by browser cookie at their first search to either conventional semantic search results or the same results with an AI answer on top, citing up to five archive articles. Nothing else differed. Treatment readers spent 58.3% more time on search pages, and the authors report confidence intervals on every headline result.

But look at what the treatment group was doing. Someone searching a newspaper's archive wants an article. The AI answer could summarize what the coverage said, but the reader came for the coverage, and every citation was a Post article: precisely the thing they were looking for. The citation was the destination.

A Google user asking an informational question mostly wants the answer. Once the summary gives it, the linked source has little left to offer. Pew's sample is everyday Google browsing, and Seer's dataset is explicitly informational and educational queries, the category where an AI answer is most complete on its own.

That reading has a second, independent piece of support. Shopify reported that half of AI-referred sessions land straight on a product page, [2.5 times the rate of traditional search](/blog/ai-referral-traffic-conversion). When an engine refers a visitor at all, it is disproportionately to a page the visitor needs to act on, which is a destination.

The authors make the narrower version of this point themselves: the study ran "within one publisher's archive," and may not generalize to engines drawing on many publishers. Three more differences cut the same way. The Post's answers cited at most five sources, all from one site, so there was no competition between domains for the click. The experiment ran in late 2024 and the Pew panel in March 2025, both before AI answers reached their [current share of Google searches](/blog/ai-overviews-share-of-searches). And Pew counts per page visit while the Post counts per search, so a reader who searched three times shows up differently in each.

## What a citation recovers, and what it doesn't

Seer's numbers are the most useful for anyone deciding whether to chase a citation on Google, and the most commonly misread. The headline, "35% higher organic CTR when cited," is accurate. The baseline is the problem.

| Seer, Q3 2025 | Organic CTR | Paid CTR |
|---|---|---|
| No AI Overview | 1.45% | 13.88% |
| AI Overview, brand cited | 0.70% | 7.89% |
| AI Overview, brand not cited | 0.52% | 4.14% |

Take the gap between "no overview" and "overview, not cited" as what the overview costs a brand. Being cited recovers 0.18 of those 0.93 points of organic CTR, about **19%**. On paid it recovers about 38%. A citation on an informational query is worth having. It does not get you back to where you were.

Seer is also explicit that the arrow may point the other way: "it's equally possible that brands with stronger authority and higher baseline CTRs are simply more likely to be cited by Google's AI." A brand that wins citations is probably a brand people already click. That makes 19% an upper bound on what winning the citation would add for a site that is not currently cited.

The only party with complete data has made a stronger claim. Announcing AI Overviews in May 2024, Google's head of Search wrote that ["the links included in AI Overviews get more clicks than if the page had appeared as a traditional web listing for that query."](https://blog.google/products/search/generative-ai-google-search-may-2024/) That can be true per link and still fit Pew's 1%, because it compares a cited page against the same page as an ordinary listing, not against the clicks the query used to send. Google has not published figures behind it, and its new Search Console AI report [shows impressions only](/blog/search-console-ai-visibility), with no clicks. Until it does, the claim cannot be checked.

## AI answers move clicks rather than simply removing them

The finding in the Washington Post data most likely to surprise marketers is not about citations at all. With the AI answer on, article consumption per search fell 18.2%, and follow-up searches more than doubled, from 10.4% to 21.6% of next actions. Readers searched 29.4% more overall, and article opens per reader ended up 4.9% higher than in the control group.

On an owned search box, an AI answer turned one search into several. Each answer prompted another question, and some of those questions ended in a click. That matches what we described in [multi-turn AI search](/blog/multi-turn-ai-search): the unit of behavior is the session, not the query. A brand that is cited on the opening question and absent from the follow-ups has missed the turns where the remaining clicks happen.

The authors also checked whether the AI answer funneled attention to already-popular articles. It did not: cited sources showed no statistically significant difference in popularity rank from the conventional results returned for the same queries. On this evidence, the citation slot is not reserved for the biggest names, which is a better result for challenger brands than most commentary assumes.

## What this does not prove

**The cleanest experiment is the least like Google.** One publisher, one site's articles, at most five citations, no competing domains. It establishes that people will click citations when the cited page is what they want. It does not give you a citation click rate for ChatGPT or AI Overviews.

**The Google numbers are correlational and dated.** Pew's panel is from March 2025 and Seer's quarter ends September 2025. Neither randomized anything, and both predate the current scale of AI answers. Pew's 1% is also an average across every query type, so it will understate destination queries and overstate pure fact lookups.

**Nobody has measured citation clicks on ChatGPT, Perplexity, or Claude** with comparable rigor. Referral logs show only the clicks that happened and arrive with no record of how many answers were shown, so no public dataset gives a click-through rate for citations on those engines.

## A decision rule for your prompt set

Read together, the three datasets support one practical rule: **estimate what a citation is worth by whether the reader needs your page, not by a single citation CTR.**

1. **Sort tracked queries into destination and evidence queries.** "Best invoicing software for contractors" and "invoice template for contractors" are destination queries: the reader has to leave the answer to act. "What is net 30" is an evidence query: the answer is the product.
2. **On destination queries, expect the citation to compete for real traffic.** The Washington Post case, where the cited page was the goal, is the closest model: citations took about a third of result clicks. These are the queries where being cited, and being the page cited, pays off in sessions.
3. **On evidence queries, expect roughly one click in nine or fewer, and value the citation as exposure.** If nobody clicks, all the citation can do is put your name in front of the reader. That depends on whether the answer names you as well as linking you, which ChatGPT skipped in about four of five appearances in the data we covered in [ghost citations](/blog/ghost-citations). An unnamed citation on an evidence query is worth very little.
4. **Never pitch a citation as recovering lost traffic on informational queries.** On Seer's numbers it recovers about a fifth, at best. Make the case on presence in the answer, and report referral sessions as supporting evidence.
